Meridian Hill Park

An Italian Garden in the Heart of D.C.

Meridian Hill Park is a formal, terraced park laid out in the Neoclassical and Italian Renaissance styles — a rare example of that tradition in American public landscape design. Its thirteen-tiered cascading fountain, the longest of its kind in North America, runs the length of the park's upper reservoir wall down to the statuary terrace below.

Since the 1960s the park has also been known informally as Malcolm X Park, a name adopted by neighborhood activists and still used widely by D.C. residents alongside its official title. It remains a gathering place for the surrounding Columbia Heights and Adams Morgan communities.

“One of the finest examples of early twentieth-century urban landscape architecture in the United States.”
— National Register of Historic Places nomination

History

  • 1819 The land is named Meridian Hill after a stone marker once placed there to record the exact longitude of the White House.
  • 1910s–1936 Landscape architects George Burnap, Horace Peaslee, and sculptor Carl Milles design and build the park in phases, with construction overseen by the Office of Public Buildings and Grounds.
  • 1936 The park officially opens to the public in its present form.
  • 1960s–70s Civil rights and Pan-African activists begin holding rallies and drum circles in the park, and the community name “Malcolm X Park” takes hold.
  • 1994 Listed on the National Register of Historic Places and designated a National Historic Landmark for its landscape architecture.
  • Today A centerpiece of Northwest D.C. life — home to the long-running Sunday drum circle, pickup soccer, picnics, and neighborhood events.

Park Features

The park is split into two distinct halves, connected by stairs and the great cascade.

The Cascading Fountain

Thirteen basins step down the hillside in the longest cascading fountain in North America, the visual spine of the entire park.

Upper Park

A formal terrace with a reflecting pool, allées of trees, and sweeping views south over the cascade toward the lower park.

Lower Park

Open lawns, mature trees, and shaded paths that host the Sunday drum circle, sports, and informal gatherings.

Statuary

Notable works include a statue of Joan of Arc, a bust of Dante, and the Serenity/Falling Waters fountain figure by Carl Milles.

Architecture

Concrete cast to resemble stone, balustrades, and formal stairs give the park a distinctly European, Beaux-Arts character.

Sunday Drum Circle

A decades-old tradition of African drumming and dance gathers in the park most Sunday afternoons, weather permitting.

Getting There

The park sits between 15th and 16th Streets NW, bordered by Euclid Street to the north and W Street to the south, straddling the Columbia Heights and Adams Morgan neighborhoods.

By Metro

Columbia Heights (Green/Yellow Line), then a short walk east.

By Bus

Metrobus routes along 16th Street and Columbia Road stop within a few blocks of the park.

By Car

Limited metered street parking is available nearby; weekends near the drum circle can fill up quickly.
